5 Lessons Japanese Executives Can Learn from Indian Business Culture

2026-10-11

Bridging Two Great Economic Powers

When Japanese multinationals expand into India, the challenge is rarely technical. It is cultural. Japan’s business ethos is built on precision, consensus, and long-term stability. India’s commercial landscape thrives on agility, relationship depth, and adaptive problem-solving.

For Japanese executives leading Global Capability Centers (GCCs) or joint ventures in India, understanding these differences is not just about avoiding friction—it is about unlocking performance. The goal is not to change one culture to match the other, but to create a hybrid operating model that leverages the strengths of both.

Here are five practical lessons from Indian business culture that can enhance the effectiveness of Japanese leadership.

1. Prioritize Relationship Depth Over Transactional Efficiency

In Japan, trust is often built through consistent, low-friction interactions and shared history. In India, trust is frequently established through personal connection and face-to-face engagement. Business is relational before it is contractual.

Japanese executives may find the initial emphasis on social rapport—discussions about family, background, or shared interests—unnecessary. However, this is the foundation of local trust. When local teams feel personally valued, they commit more deeply to shared goals.

Consider scheduling regular informal check-ins that are not strictly agenda-driven. This does not mean abandoning structure; it means recognizing that in India, the quality of the relationship directly influences the quality of the output. A strong personal bond reduces the need for micromanagement later.

2. Embrace "Jugaad" as Creative Problem-Solving

The term jugaad is often translated as "frugal innovation," but it goes deeper. It represents a mindset of finding flexible, low-cost, and immediate solutions to complex constraints. It is not about cutting corners; it is about bypassing bureaucratic inertia to get things done.

Japanese management principles, such as kaizen (continuous improvement), share a philosophical kinship with jugaad. Both value incremental progress. However, jugaad is often more improvisational. It thrives in environments where standard operating procedures may not account for local realities.

"Jugaad is not a substitute for quality; it is a bridge to it when resources are constrained. It teaches leaders to focus on the outcome, not just the process."

Encourage local teams to propose unconventional solutions. Create a safe space for experimentation where the focus is on the result rather than strict adherence to predefined methods. This fosters innovation and empowers local talent.

3. Value Speed and Adaptability in Decision-Making

Japanese decision-making often follows the nemawashi process—building consensus before formal approval. This ensures buy-in and minimizes risk, but it can be slow. In India, market dynamics change rapidly, and waiting for perfect consensus can mean missing opportunities.

Indian business culture often values decisive action based on available information, with the willingness to adjust course as new data emerges. This agility is a competitive advantage in sectors like technology, fintech, and digital services.

Executives can learn to balance thoroughness with speed. Implement "decision windows" where local leaders are empowered to make quick calls within defined boundaries. This reduces bottlenecks and signals trust in local expertise. It also aligns with the faster pace of the Indian market, where responsiveness is often rewarded.

4. Leverage Hierarchical Respect with Flat Communication

Both Japanese and Indian cultures have high power distance, meaning hierarchy is respected. However, the expression of this hierarchy differs. In Japan, hierarchy is often implicit and procedural. In India, it is more overt and personal.

While respecting hierarchy is important, excessive deference can stifle open communication. Indian teams may hesitate to challenge senior leaders, even when they have critical insights. This can lead to unresolved issues and suboptimal outcomes.

Japanese executives can foster a culture of "respectful dissent." Encourage junior staff to share their views in structured forums, ensuring that hierarchy does not silence valuable input. Recognize that in India, giving face is important, but so is demonstrating that diverse perspectives are valued. This balance builds psychological safety while maintaining respect.

5. Invest in Long-Term Talent Development

India has a vast, young, and ambitious talent pool. However, retention and skill development are critical challenges. Japanese companies often have strong training frameworks, but these may not resonate with Indian employees who value rapid career progression and diverse experiences.

Indian professionals often seek roles that offer clear growth paths and exposure to global projects. They value recognition and the opportunity to work on high-impact initiatives. A one-size-fits-all approach to career development will not work.

Invest in personalized career roadmaps. Provide mentorship opportunities that connect local talent with global leaders. Highlight the impact of their work on the broader organization. When Indian employees see a clear future within the company, they become long-term partners rather than transient contributors.

Conclusion: Building a Hybrid Advantage

The synergy between Japanese precision and Indian agility is a powerful combination. It is not about choosing one culture over the other, but about integrating their strengths. By prioritizing relationships, embracing creative problem-solving, speeding up decisions, balancing hierarchy with open communication, and investing in talent, Japanese executives can lead more effectively in India.

This approach does not just improve operational efficiency; it builds a resilient, adaptive organization capable of navigating global complexities. The future of cross-border business lies in this hybrid model.
