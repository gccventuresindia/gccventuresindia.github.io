The shadow AI challenge: safeguarding business data while enabling innovation Shadow AI: A Practical Playbook for leadership community for protecting data way forward Employees across organizations are already using generative AI to draft content and emails, analyse information, write code, and streamline routine work including automating some tasks with agentic AI. The leadership question is no longer whether AI will be used at work or whether employees will use approved tools. It’s whether the organization can set clear guardrails to guide its use before sensitive data or business processes are put at risk. Even when companies have controls designed to block sensitive data from reaching unapproved AI tools, how consistently do those controls cover every tool, device, and workflow? Shadow AI can still create blind spots when employees use services the organization can’t fully monitor—for example, by pasting customer information into a chatbot, uploading an internal document, or connecting an AI assistant to company files. The risk depends on whether those actions are detected or prevented, and how the service handles any data that gets through. For CISOs and business leaders, the objective should be clear: reduce unacceptable exposure while enabling approved, measurable use. What should leaders do now? 1. Establish a short-term policy employees can act on. Set clear, practical rules employees can follow. Specify what must never be entered into unapproved AI tools such as credentials, regulated personal information, customer data, source code, or confidential business information. Keep the guidance easy to find and provide a straightforward process for requesting an exception. 2. Find out and design controls post identifying use cases being currently used. Use appropriate security signals, purchasing records, employee feedback, and conversations with business teams to build a clear picture of the tools and tasks in use. Focus first on higher-risk situations, like AI tools connected to sensitive data or customer-facing work. Be open about any monitoring and respect privacy requirements. 3. Give teams safe tools / approved tools for the work they need to do. Start with a small set of approved AI tools that meet business needs and match the level of risk. Before rollout, check how each tool handles and stores data, whether it uses data for training, and how it manages access, integrations, contracts, and security incidents. Put the right protections in place rather than relying on a vendor’s assurances. 4. Put ownership and approval paths in place. Assign clear responsibilities across security, IT, privacy, legal, procurement, and business teams. Set a lightweight review process for new tools and integrations, with risk-based approval timelines so low-risk pilots are not trapped in lengthy reviews. 5. Set controls around data and connections. Apply least-privilege access, identity controls, and restrictions on plugins or connectors. Where feasible, use technical controls to prevent sensitive data from being sent to unapproved services. Give extra scrutiny to AI agents that can read, modify, or transmit information across business systems. 6. Prepare for mistakes and measure progress. Create a straightforward reporting process for accidental data sharing, with clear steps for containment and escalation. Track practical measures such as approved-tool adoption, time to review requests, high-risk use cases remediated and reported exposure events. Review these measures regularly and adjust controls as products and business needs change. The leadership test A useful shadow-AI program should answer three questions: Do we know where AI is being used for work? Do employees have an approved way to meet their highest-priority needs? Can we detect, contain, and learn from unsafe use? AI governance is not a one-time policy exercise. It is an operating capability that helps the organization make informed decisions about data, risk, and business value. The goal is not to eliminate AI use. It is to make responsible use the easiest path and to ensure leaders can see whether the safeguards are working.
