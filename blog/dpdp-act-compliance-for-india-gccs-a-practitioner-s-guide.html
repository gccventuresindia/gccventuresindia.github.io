DPDP Act Compliance for India GCCs: A Practitioner’s Guide

2026-10-23

The New Compliance Imperative for Global Capability Centers

The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023, marks a definitive shift in India’s regulatory landscape for Global Capability Centers (GCCs). For years, GCCs have operated under a patchwork of sector-specific guidelines and international standards like GDPR, often treating local data laws as secondary to global mandates. That era is over. The DPDP Act establishes a unified, principles-based framework that applies directly to any entity processing personal data within India, regardless of where the parent organization is headquartered.

For GCC leaders, this is not merely a legal checkbox exercise; it is a structural imperative. GCCs are increasingly becoming hubs for high-value data processing, from customer analytics to employee management and R&D support. As these centers handle significant volumes of Indian residents’ data, they sit at the center of regulatory scrutiny. Compliance is no longer optional for market access; it is foundational to operational continuity. This article outlines the practical steps GCCs must take to align their data governance with the new law, focusing on actionable strategy rather than theoretical compliance.

Understanding the Scope: Who Is Covered?

The first step in compliance is accurately defining the scope of applicability. The DPDP Act applies to the processing of digital personal data within India. Crucially, it also extends to the processing of such data by entities outside India, provided the processing is connected to the profiling or interactive behavioral tracking of individuals within India.

For GCCs, this distinction is vital. Most GCCs serve global headquarters and process data for users in India, making them squarely within the Act’s extraterritorial reach. This means that a GCC operating as a shared service center for a US-based tech firm is not exempt simply because the final decision-maker is abroad. The moment the GCC processes data of Indian residents, the obligations under the DPDP Act attach.

Practitioners must also distinguish between "Data Fiduciaries" and "Data Processors." In many GCC structures, the global parent is the Data Fiduciary (the entity determining the purpose and means of processing), while the GCC may act as a Data Processor (processing data on behalf of the fiduciary). However, if the GCC independently determines the purpose of processing—for instance, by conducting local market research or managing local employee data—it may be classified as a Data Fiduciary in its own right. This classification carries direct liability, which GCC leaders must account for in their risk matrices.

Appointing a Significant Data Fiduciary Representative

One of the most immediate operational changes for larger GCCs is the requirement to appoint a Representative in India. If a GCC is classified as a "Significant Data Fiduciary" based on criteria such as the volume of data processed, the sensitivity of the data, and the potential risk to the rights of data principals, it must designate a representative within India.

This representative acts as the primary point of contact for the Data Protection Board of India and for data principals seeking to exercise their rights. For GCCs, this role is critical. It ensures that there is a localized entity capable of responding to regulatory inquiries and individual grievances in a timely manner.

From a governance perspective, GCCs should view this appointment not just as a regulatory requirement but as a mechanism for local accountability. The representative should be integrated into the GCC’s compliance team, with clear reporting lines to both the local GCC leadership and the global privacy office. This dual reporting structure ensures that local nuances are captured while maintaining alignment with global privacy strategies.

Mapping Data Flows and Consent Mechanisms

At the heart of the DPDP Act is the principle of consent. Data fiduciaries must obtain free, specific, informed, unconditional, and revocable consent before processing personal data. For GCCs, this requires a rigorous audit of existing data flows and consent architectures.

GCCs often inherit legacy systems and processes that may not meet the stringent standards of the DPDP Act. Practitioners should begin by mapping all data flows involving Indian residents. This includes identifying where data enters the GCC, how it is stored, processed, and shared, and where it exits. This mapping exercise should cover both explicit consent mechanisms (such as web forms) and implicit data collection (such as cookies or transactional data).

Once flows are mapped, GCCs must assess the adequacy of their consent mechanisms. The Act requires that consent be distinguishable from other terms and conditions, easily accessible, and granular. GCCs should review their user interfaces and internal HR systems to ensure that consent is not bundled with general service agreements. Furthermore, mechanisms for withdrawing consent must be as easy to exercise as giving it. For GCCs managing global databases, this may require technical updates to ensure that a withdrawal of consent in India triggers the appropriate de-provisioning or anonymization of data across all systems.

Implementing Data Principal Rights

The DPDP Act grants significant rights to data principals (the individuals whose data is being processed). These include the right to registration, the right to access information, the right to correction, the right to erasure, and the right to grievous redressal. GCCs, as processors or fiduciaries, play a key role in facilitating these rights.

Practitioners must establish robust internal processes to handle requests from data principals. This involves creating clear channels for individuals to submit requests and defining SLAs for response. For example, if an Indian resident requests the erasure of their data, the GCC must have the technical capability to locate and delete that data from all active and backup systems, while also notifying any third parties with whom the data was shared.

It is also important to note the limitations on these rights. For instance, the right to erasure may be subject to exceptions for compliance with legal obligations or the discovery, ascertainment, or prosecution of legal offenses. GCCs must document these exceptions clearly and ensure that their legal teams are aligned on when these exemptions apply. Transparency is key; data principals should be informed of any limitations on their rights and the reasons for such limitations.

Data Protection and Security Obligations

While the DPDP Act is principles-based regarding security, it mandates that Data Fiduciaries implement reasonable security practices and procedures to protect personal data. For GCCs, this means leveraging existing global security frameworks while ensuring they meet local expectations.

Practitioners should conduct a gap analysis of their current security controls against the requirements of the Act and any subsequent rules issued by the government. This includes assessing physical, technical, and administrative safeguards. Key areas of focus include data encryption, access controls, incident response protocols, and vendor management.

Given the cross-border nature of GCC operations, special attention must be paid to data transfers. While the DPDP Act allows for the free flow of personal data across borders, it does not prohibit restrictions. GCCs must ensure that any international data transfers are supported by adequate contractual safeguards and that the data protection standards in the destination country are comparable to those in India. Regular audits and penetration testing should be integrated into the GCC’s routine security operations to demonstrate due diligence.

Building a Culture of Compliance

Technical and legal compliance is only half the battle; the other half is cultural. GCCs must foster a culture of privacy awareness among their employees. This involves regular training for staff who handle personal data, ensuring they understand their roles and responsibilities under the DPDP Act.

Leadership within the GCC must champion this initiative. When employees see that privacy is a priority from the top down, they are more likely to adhere to protocols and report potential breaches. GCCs should establish clear policies for data minimization and retention, ensuring that data is not kept longer than necessary.

Furthermore, GCCs should consider establishing a Data Protection Officer (DPO) or a similar role dedicated to overseeing compliance. This individual should have the authority to act independently and report directly to senior management. The DPO can serve as an internal advocate for privacy, helping to integrate compliance into product development and business decisions from the outset.

Conclusion: From Compliance to Competitive Advantage

Compliance with the DPDP Act is not a one-time project but an ongoing journey. For GCCs, the initial focus should be on understanding the scope, mapping data flows, and establishing robust consent and security mechanisms. As the regulatory framework matures, GCCs will need to stay agile, adapting to new rules and guidance from the Data Protection Board.

However, there is an opportunity here beyond mere compliance. By building strong, transparent, and secure data governance frameworks, GCCs can enhance trust with their global stakeholders and local customers. A robust privacy posture can differentiate a GCC in the eyes of potential clients and partners, signaling a commitment to ethical data practices.

GCC leaders are encouraged to begin their compliance journey by conducting a thorough audit of their current data practices. Engaging with legal counsel and privacy experts early in the process can help identify gaps and develop a tailored roadmap. As you navigate this new landscape, remember that compliance is not just about avoiding penalties; it is about building a foundation for sustainable, responsible growth in India’s digital economy.

"For GCCs, the DPDP Act is not just a regulatory hurdle but a blueprint for building trust. By prioritizing data principal rights and robust security, GCCs can transform compliance into a competitive advantage."
