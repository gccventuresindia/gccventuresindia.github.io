THE SHADOW AI CHALLENGE

Safeguarding business data while enabling innovation — a practical playbook for the leadership community.

By Jacob · 5 Oct 2026 · 7 min read

Employees across organizations are already using generative AI to draft content and emails, analyse information, write code, and streamline routine work — including automating some tasks with agentic AI.

The leadership question is no longer whether AI will be used at work, or whether employees will use approved tools. It’s whether the organization can set clear guardrails to guide its use before sensitive data or business processes are put at risk.

For CISOs and business leaders, the objective should be clear: reduce unacceptable exposure while enabling approved, measurable use.

01 | Where The Blind Spots Appear

Even when companies have controls designed to block sensitive data from reaching unapproved AI tools, how consistently do those controls cover every tool, device, and workflow?

Shadow AI still creates blind spots when employees use services the organization can’t fully monitor — for example, by pasting customer information into a chatbot, uploading an internal document, or connecting an AI assistant to company files.

The risk depends on whether those actions are detected or prevented, and how the service handles any data that gets through.

02 | Six Moves Leaders Should Make Now

01 — Establish a short-term policy employees can act on

Set clear, practical rules employees can follow. Specify what must never be entered into unapproved AI tools — credentials, regulated personal information, customer data, source code, or confidential business information. Keep the guidance easy to find, and provide a straightforward process for requesting an exception.

02 — Find the use cases in flight, then design controls

Use appropriate security signals, purchasing records, employee feedback, and conversations with business teams to build a clear picture of the tools and tasks in use. Focus first on higher-risk situations — AI tools connected to sensitive data, or customer-facing work. Be open about any monitoring, and respect privacy requirements.

03 — Give teams safe, approved tools for the work they need to do

Start with a small set of approved AI tools that meet business needs and match the level of risk. Before rollout, check how each tool handles and stores data, whether it uses data for training, and how it manages access, integrations, contracts, and security incidents. Put the right protections in place rather than relying on a vendor’s assurances.

04 — Put ownership and approval paths in place

Assign clear responsibilities across security, IT, privacy, legal, procurement, and business teams. Set a lightweight review process for new tools and integrations, with risk-based approval timelines so low-risk pilots are not trapped in lengthy reviews.

05 — Set controls around data and connections

Apply least-privilege access, identity controls, and restrictions on plugins or connectors. Where feasible, use technical controls to prevent sensitive data from being sent to unapproved services. Give extra scrutiny to AI agents that can read, modify, or transmit information across business systems.

06 — Prepare for mistakes and measure progress

Create a straightforward reporting process for accidental data sharing, with clear steps for containment and escalation. Track practical measures, review them regularly, and adjust controls as products and business needs change.

Measure What it tells you
01 APPROVED-TOOL ADOPTION Whether the safe path is being used, or employees are still going around it.
02 TIME TO REVIEW REQUESTS Whether the approval process is fast enough to be useful rather than an obstacle.
03 HIGH-RISK CASES REMEDIATED Whether identified exposures are actually being closed, not just logged.
04 REPORTED EXPOSURE EVENTS Whether employees are telling you early, which is the behaviour you want to build.

03 | The Leadership Test

A useful shadow-AI program should answer three questions. Leadership can use this list to assess where the organization stands today:

01. VISIBILITY
Do we know where AI is being used for work?

If the answer is no, every other control is built on an assumption rather than evidence.

02. ACCESS
Do employees have an approved way to meet their highest-priority needs?

Shadow AI thrives in the gap between what the policy forbids and what people are actually required to deliver.

03. RESPONSE
Can we detect, contain, and learn from unsafe use?

Detection without containment is reporting. Containment without learning is an incident, not a control.

AI governance is not a one-time policy exercise. It is an operating capability that helps the organization make informed decisions about data, risk, and business value.
